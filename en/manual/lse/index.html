01 · overview

What was LSE

LSE stands for Langage Symbolique d’Enseignement, or Symbolic Teaching Language. It was a French project from the second half of the twentieth century aimed at introducing programming through teachable symbolic notation. Educational publications from the 1970s describe the language as a teaching tool at a time when computer access often involved time-sharing systems.

LSE matters as a historical experiment in programming education. It should not be confused with a modern general-purpose language or a currently maintained software distribution.

Status in Potoo IDE: LSE is under evaluation. This page is a historical reference; it does not claim current support or an interpreter in the app.

02 · historical context

Development context

Publications from Enseignement Public et Informatique document the presentation of LSE from 1973 onward and discuss its place in teaching. The proposal was to provide symbolic vocabulary for explaining instructions, data, and algorithm structure in French.

The technical context matters when interpreting the material: computers were shared among users, and interaction with a system did not resemble a contemporary graphical editor. Manuals and exercises also reflect the educational practices and available resources of that period.

  • Purpose: support the teaching of programming concepts.
  • Language: notation and teaching materials in French.
  • Period: development and adoption associated with the 1960s and 1970s.
  • Current status: the sources consulted are historical, not an active implementation portal.

03 · documented concepts

What the sources record

Historical accounts describe a textual language for expressing operations and procedures. The secondary material consulted mentions data, strings, routines, and control structures, including the ability to decompose a solution into procedures and recursive calls.

TopicHistorical reading
Instruction sequenceExpress ordered operations for solving a problem.
Data and expressionsRepresent values and calculations in symbolic notation.
ControlOrganize choices and repetitions in algorithms.
ProceduresSplit a solution into reusable routines; some sources also report recursion.
InteractionStudy data input and output in the context of shared systems.

This summary is not a formal specification: each detail needs confirmation in the relevant period manual before it is treated as a language rule.

04 · syntax and sources

Why this page has no program listing

The historical files found do not form one current specification against which a complete example can be checked. Reserved-word spellings, delimiters, types, and execution rules may differ between materials and periods.

For that reason, this reference does not present invented pseudocode under the LSE name. To study a period listing:

  1. record the publication, date, and environment version it belongs to;
  2. transcribe the example faithfully, preserving symbols and accents;
  3. consult the explanation that accompanies the listing;
  4. do not assume a current compiler accepts the syntax unchanged.

This keeps documented history separate from a specification the sources consulted do not establish.

05 · teaching approach

Reading historical exercises

LSE exercises should be read as teaching material from their period. Instead of first asking which current language can run a file, look for the concept being taught: sequence, calculation, branching, repetition, or decomposition into routines.

  • Identify the given data and expected result.
  • Mark instructions that change state and those that only display information.
  • Trace a short execution by hand.
  • Separate the algorithmic idea from the exact spelling used in that manual edition.

A modern reimplementation may help study the algorithm, but it must be identified as a translation, not native LSE execution.

06 · documentary limits

Reference status

This documentation relies on historical texts available through educational archives and an encyclopedic overview. No active official site with a current distribution fixing grammar and supported tools was found.

As a result, detailed features, differences between versions, and the availability of simulators remain unconfirmed here. The lack of an accessible specification does not mean period materials never existed; it means each original publication must be consulted before making precise claims.

07 · study guide

A research and reading checklist

When using LSE in historical research, cite the document and its date rather than only naming the language. Keep a copy of the source and note whether a transcription came from an article, a book, or software documentation.

  • Compare how LSE is described with the language the publication teaches.
  • Distinguish conceptual explanations from code listings.
  • Do not mix keywords from Portugol, BASIC, or another pseudolanguage into examples.
  • If you translate an exercise, name the target language and explain the changes.

This checklist matters because the project is historical and current pages do not provide one source for validating every syntax detail.

8 · reading the sources

Fragments documented in the historical publication

Jacques Hebenstreit and Yves Noyelle's article describes specific language features. The excerpts below come from the publication reproduced by EPI; they are not a complete program or an executable specification:

CHAINE DATE
DATE = '11 AVRIL 1972'

X = 1
REMISE = 'RABAIS 1 DE' ! X

The article documents string variables declared with CHAINE, literals in apostrophes, and the ! concatenation operator. It also describes SCH for extracting a substring and ALE(1) for producing a random value between zero and one, excluding both endpoints.

FeatureWhat the source records
Numeric valuesArithmetic variables are real; the article does not describe a separate integer type.
MemoryLIBERER makes space used by variables or arrays available again.
StringsCHAINE, apostrophe-delimited constants, concatenation with !, and extraction with SCH.
AbstractionProcedures, functions of reasonable complexity, and recursion are cited as teaching features.

Read the names and examples in the context of the period article. An isolated fragment does not establish every delimiter, declaration, or execution rule, so this page does not present it as code ready for a current compiler.

Line-based interaction in the described system

The retrospective article describes a system that analyzed each line as it was entered. The notation and commands below belong to that historical account; interfaces and features varied across machines.

Cited elementDocumented roleInterpretation limit
? instructionRuns an instruction immediately in desk-machine mode.This is a documented interaction mode, not the start of a numbered program.
number instructionStores a program line for later execution.The line limit was 80 characters, including the line ending.
Line analysisThe system analyzed each entered line and translated it to a postfix string.The source uses “compilation” pedagogically; it also describes an interpreter.
PAUSEInterrupts execution and reports the line where it stopped.The historical monitor provided commands to continue or resume elsewhere.
PAS A PASStops execution after each line.This is a historical monitor feature, not a modern graphical debugger.

The publication also cites LIRE, AFFICHER, SI ... ALORS ... SINON, ALLER EN, and forms of FAIRE. Explicit loop structures are associated with later revisions; do not combine all these forms into one example without naming the source edition.

9 · technical context

Teaching criteria and technical context

The authors explain that French reserved words were intended to keep students from having to use English to communicate with a computer. The proposal also aimed to work across school subjects, serve students in both scientific and literary fields, and provide conversational interaction during compilation and execution.

The design paired a minimal core that was easy to learn with advanced features for exploring procedures, pass-by-value and pass-by-name parameters, recursion, and functions. This reflects two teaching goals: support simple first experiences while also discussing decomposition and abstraction without changing languages.

The article places the implementation on a shared minicomputer with 16 kilobytes of memory, a 100-kilobyte disk, and eight terminals. Those limits help explain its discussion of freeing memory and time-sharing systems. They describe the period's environment, not the requirements of a current installation.

To study an LSE exercise today, separate three layers: the algorithmic concept; the exact notation shown by the source; and the material conditions of the system in which it ran. Preserve French terms when transcribing examples, mark rules the source does not explain, and describe the logic in prose when the available grammar is insufficient for a complete program.

  1. Identify the source's author, publication, and historical context.
  2. Mark each instruction, type, or function that is actually illustrated.
  3. Compare later accounts with the period article without treating them as interchangeable.
  4. If you adapt an exercise to a modern language, label the adaptation explicitly.
